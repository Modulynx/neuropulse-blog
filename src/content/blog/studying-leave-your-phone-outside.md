It's 8:40 on a Tuesday night. The textbook is open, the highlighter is uncapped, and your phone is face down next to your mug. It hasn't buzzed once. You still read the same paragraph three times.

If that sounds familiar, try this next time: leave your phone outside the room. Switched off, not just silenced. Here's why it may help.

## Why does my focus slip when my phone is right there?

Behind your forehead sits the part of your brain that plans, holds your goal, and keeps you on task. Think of it as your planning center.

When your phone is within reach, that planning center may have an extra job. It has to keep not picking the phone up. That's a bit like a browser tab you never opened but can't stop feeling in the background. Nothing is happening on screen, yet part of your attention stays tied to it.

That quiet tug is hard to notice. You just feel like your focus is leaking.

## What did the study actually find?

In a 2023 study by Skowronek and colleagues, published in Scientific Reports, 42 adults did an attention test under different phone conditions. Some had their phone switched off and left outside the room. Others had a phone lying on the desk.

The people with the phone outside scored higher, about 109 compared with about 100. They also worked faster.

That's a real gap, but it's one small study. We don't know yet how well it holds up over a long study session, or how it plays out for every kind of student or worker. It also doesn't tell us how a phone in a bag or on silent compares. What it does suggest is that having the phone out of sight and out of the room may make it easier to keep your attention on the page.

## What's one simple habit to try tonight?

Switch your phone off and leave it outside the room while you study. That's the whole habit.

Here's a mini plan for today:

1. **Switch it off before you sit down.** Fully off, so there's no buzz to wonder about.
2. **Put it in another room.** The kitchen counter, a coat pocket by the front door, or a bag in the hallway all work.
3. **Pick a stopping point.** Maybe the end of a chapter or a set of practice problems. Check your phone then, not before.

If you use your phone as a timer, grab a cheap kitchen timer or an old watch instead. It's a small swap, and it keeps the screen out of the picture.

The first ten minutes may feel itchy. That's normal. Most people just reach for a phone that isn't there a few times and then settle.

## Who should be careful?

This habit is simple, but a few situations call for a bit of thought.

If you're pregnant, on medication, or have a heart condition, your phone might be tied to health things like alarms, reminders, or a call to your care team. Don't switch it off if you need it for that. Talk with your clinician about what works for you.

If you feel real anxiety when you can't be reached, going fully off may feel stressful. You could tell a family member where you'll be and for how long, or start with a short stretch.

And if you're on call or caring for someone, keep a way to be reached. A better study session isn't worth missing something important.

## FAQ

### Should I leave my phone in another room while studying?

It may help. In a 2023 study of 42 adults, people whose phone was switched off and left outside the room scored higher on an attention test and worked faster than people with a phone on the desk. It's one small study, so treat it as something to try, not a promise.

### Does a phone on silent still distract you?

The study we covered compared a switched-off phone outside the room with a phone lying on the desk. It can't tell us how silent mode alone compares. If you notice yourself glancing at the phone, moving it out of the room is an easy thing to test.

### What can I use instead of my phone as a study timer?

A kitchen timer, a basic watch, or a small desk clock all work. You get the timing without a screen full of notifications sitting next to your textbook.
