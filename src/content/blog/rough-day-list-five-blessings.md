It's 9:40 pm. The dishes are done, a mug of tea is going cold on the counter, and you're back in the 3 pm meeting. You're replaying one comment, your answer, and the better answer you didn't give.

The day is over, but your head hasn't gotten the message. Tonight, a small habit might help: **list five blessings** on paper.

## Why does a rough day keep replaying in your head?

Because your brain treats a stressful moment as unfinished business. It keeps going back to check what went wrong and how to avoid it next time.

That's useful when there's a real problem to solve. It's less useful at 9:40 pm when you're just trying to relax.

## What's happening in your brain when you can't let go?

Think of your brain's alarm center as a smoke detector that keeps beeping after the toast is already in the trash. It's doing its job, but the threat is gone. It still sounds busy.

Looking for what went right may help. When you deliberately notice good things, you give your attention somewhere else to land.

We should be honest here. We don't know exactly what changes in the brain when someone writes a gratitude list. The research we're looking at measured how people felt, not what showed up on a scan.

## What does research say when you list five blessings?

A 2003 study by Emmons and McCullough, published in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, looked at this kind of habit. People wrote up to five things they were grateful for once a week for ten weeks.

By the end, they felt better about their lives. They were also more optimistic about the coming week.

That's encouraging, but it's one study. It doesn't mean a list will lift everyone's mood, and it doesn't mean one bad night will turn around after one list. Treat it as a promising idea to test, not a promise.

Notice how small the habit was. It was once a week, and no more than five lines. Nobody had to write a long journal entry.

## How do you list five blessings? A 3-step plan for today

You don't need an app or a fancy notebook. A sticky note works.

1. **Pick a spot.** Choose a time you'll actually remember, like Sunday evening or Friday after work. If today was rough, you can try it tonight.
2. **Write up to five lines.** Small and specific beats big and vague. "My coworker covered the phones while I ate lunch" is better than "my job." If you only get to two or three, that's fine.
3. **Read it once, then close it.** Don't grade your list or argue with it. The point is to let your mind rest on something other than the meeting.

It should take just a few minutes. If you like it, you could repeat it next week, which is roughly the rhythm people in the study followed.

## Who should be careful with this habit?

Writing a short list is low risk for most people. Still, a few notes.

If replaying the day feels constant, or comes with ongoing anxiety, a low mood that won't lift, or trouble functioning, a list isn't a substitute for real support. Please talk with a doctor or a licensed therapist.

And if something at work is truly unsafe or unfair, gratitude shouldn't be used to talk yourself out of dealing with it. You can do both.

## FAQ

### How often should you write a gratitude list?

There's no single right answer. In the 2003 study by Emmons and McCullough, people wrote up to five things once a week for ten weeks. A weekly list is a reasonable place to start, and you can adjust it to what feels doable.

### Does gratitude journaling really improve mood?

Research suggests it may help. In that study, people who wrote weekly lists felt better about their lives and more optimistic about the coming week. It's one study, though, and results can differ from person to person.

### What if I can't think of five things I'm grateful for?

That's okay. The study allowed up to five, so one or two is a fine start. Look for small, specific things from your day, like a good cup of coffee or a friendly text. If nothing comes to mind for days and your mood stays low, consider talking with a health professional.
