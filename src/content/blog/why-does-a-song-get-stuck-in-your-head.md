You're rinsing a mug at the kitchen sink. The radio went off an hour ago. The chorus of a song you don't even like is still playing, on a loop, in your head.

It's a small thing. But by mid-afternoon it can wear you down, and there's no off switch.

## Why does a song keep playing after it stops?

Short answer: your brain is doing its normal job a little too well.

The auditory cortex, the hearing part of your brain, handles every sound you hear. It doesn't only react when sound reaches your ears. With music you know well, it can keep going on its own.

## What happens in your brain when a song loops?

Think of your phone's autocomplete. You type two words and it offers the third. Your brain does something similar with songs you know: it has the tune stored, so when part of it goes missing, it fills in the rest.

## What did the study find?

A team at Dartmouth College scanned volunteers' brains while they listened to music with short silent gaps cut into it ([Kraemer et al., Nature, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1038/434158a)). When the gaps fell inside songs people knew, the auditory cortex stayed active during the silence.

In other words, the brain filled in the missing sound by itself.

## What this study can't tell us

The study looked at silent gaps in familiar music, not at stuck songs. So it helps explain how a song can keep playing with nothing coming in, but not why one song sticks and another doesn't. It also says nothing about how to stop one.

## What can you try today?

The tip below comes from a different study, so treat it as a small experiment. In three lab experiments at the University of Reading, chewing gum cut down how often people "heard" a tune in their head ([Beaman et al., Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470218.2015.1034142)). The researchers think chewing gets in the way of the silent "singing along" that keeps a tune going.

1. **Notice it without a fight.** "Oh, there's that song again." Pushing hard against a tune tends to keep it around.
2. **Chew some gum.** Sugar-free is kinder to your teeth.
3. **Give your mind one task that needs words.** Answer an email or read a page. Something that takes real attention.

## Who should be careful?

Skip gum if you have jaw pain or dental work that gum could damage, and don't give it to young children. If songs or thoughts loop so much that they cause distress, or you hear music that isn't there, talk with a doctor.

## FAQ

### Why does a song get stuck in your head?

The hearing part of your brain can stay active with no sound coming in, especially for music you know. In the study above, it kept working through silent gaps in familiar songs. It's a normal brain habit, not a sign of a problem.

### Does chewing gum help?

In one set of lab experiments, chewing gum reduced how often people heard a tune in their head. It's cheap and safe to try, but it isn't guaranteed.

### Is it normal to have a song stuck all day?

Yes, it's very common and usually fades by itself. If the looping is distressing or gets in the way of your day, talk with a doctor.
