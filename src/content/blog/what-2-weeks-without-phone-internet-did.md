You're on the sofa with a mug going cold on the armrest. The show is on, but your thumb is already swiping through something else. You didn't decide to pick up the phone. You just did.

It's a familiar loop, and it's why a recent study on **2 weeks without phone internet** caught our eye.

## Why does your thumb keep swiping when you want to put the phone down?

Most of us assume it's a willpower problem. We don't think that's the full picture.

A phone offers a new thing every few seconds. A new post, a new message, a new headline. Each one is a small invitation to switch.

## What is your brain doing while you scroll?

Behind your forehead sits the prefrontal cortex, which we're calling the planning center. Textbooks link it with holding a goal in mind and keeping your attention on it.

Think of it as a desk where someone keeps sliding a fresh sheet of paper over the one you're reading. You can keep pulling the first page back, but it takes effort each time.

When the planning center keeps switching, focus may feel thin. Reading a page or following a show can start to feel harder than it used to.

## What happened after 2 weeks without phone internet?

A 2025 study by Castelo and colleagues, published in PNAS Nexus, looked at this question in 467 adults. People had mobile internet blocked on their phones for two weeks.

Research suggests that the block helped sustained attention, which is the ability to stay with one thing for a while. After the two weeks, 91% of people improved on at least one measure.

That's a big share, but it has limits. "At least one measure" doesn't mean everyone improved on everything. We're also describing attention results here, not brain scans, so we can't say what changed inside anyone's head.

The study also blocked the internet for a full two weeks. We don't know yet how much a shorter break might do.

## What's a simple way to try this today?

You don't have to go all in on day one. Try a small version first and see how your focus feels.

The habit is simple: for one evening, cut your phone off from the internet, but keep calls and texts if you need them.

1. **Block it.** Turn off mobile data and Wi-Fi on your phone, or use a built-in downtime setting that limits apps. Pick a window you can stick to, like after dinner.
2. **Move it.** Put the phone in another room, or at least across the room from the sofa. If it's out of reach, your thumb has nothing to swipe.
3. **Notice.** Before bed, jot down one line on paper: how did your attention feel tonight? Do the same tomorrow if you like.

If the evening goes well, you could try a longer block. A single evening is our own scaled-down idea, not what the study tested.

## When might this not help?

Blocking the internet won't suit everyone. If you rely on your phone for work, caregiving, or safety, keep the access you need.

It may also not do much if your attention trouble has other causes, like poor sleep or heavy stress. If your focus problems last for weeks, keep getting worse, or get in the way of work or daily life, talk to a doctor or a therapist. They can look at the whole picture, which a phone break can't.

## FAQ

### Does blocking mobile internet improve attention?

Research suggests it may. In a 2025 study by Castelo and colleagues in PNAS Nexus, 467 adults had mobile internet blocked for two weeks, and sustained attention improved. After the two weeks, 91% improved on at least one measure. It's one study, so it's a promising sign, not a promise.

### What part of the brain helps you stay focused?

The prefrontal cortex, at the front of the brain, is linked with holding goals in mind and keeping attention on a task. It's thought to be one of several areas involved in focus. The study we described measured attention, not brain activity.

### Do I need to give up my phone for two weeks to see a benefit?

We don't know. The study used a two-week block, and we can't say how much a shorter break would help. A single phone-free evening is a low-risk way to see how your own focus feels. If your attention problems last or get worse, talk to a doctor or therapist.
