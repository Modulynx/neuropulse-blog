Picture two groups of adults, each on a 90-minute walk. One group follows a busy road lined with traffic. The other walks through grassland with scattered oak trees, away from the cars.

Researchers scanned everyone's brain before and after. Only the people on the nature walk showed a drop in activity in one brain area linked with brooding. If your mind tends to loop on the same worry, this one is for you.

## Why does your mind loop on the same worry?

You know the feeling. You're walking to the train, and the same awkward email replays in your head for the fifth time.

That loop has a name: brooding, or rumination. It's when your mind chews on the same problem, often about yourself, without getting anywhere. Whether the place you're in makes it easier or harder to step out of that loop is the question this study asked.

## What happens in the brain when you brood?

One area often linked with brooding is the subgenual prefrontal cortex. It sits low in the front of the brain, toward the middle. Activity there has been linked with sad, self-focused thinking and with rumination.

Think of a worry loop as a browser tab you forgot to close. It keeps running in the background while you try to do other things. That's the one image we'll use here, and it's a simplification, since brain areas work in networks and not alone.

## What did the study find about a walk under trees?

At Stanford, Gregory Bratman and colleagues worked with 38 healthy adults, none with a psychiatric diagnosis ([Bratman et al., PNAS, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1510459112)). Each person was randomly assigned to one walk. 19 walked for 90 minutes in a greenspace near Stanford, grassland with scattered oak trees and shrubs. The other 19 walked for 90 minutes along El Camino Real, a busy road in Palo Alto.

Before and after the walk, everyone filled in a questionnaire on brooding (the Reflection Rumination Questionnaire). They also had a brain scan that measured blood flow in the subgenual prefrontal cortex, a sign of how active that area is.

After the nature walk, people reported less brooding than before, and blood flow in that brain area went down. After the road walk, neither changed. Heart rate and breathing did not differ between the two groups, so the difference doesn't seem to come from one walk being harder on the body than the other.

So here the researchers measured both how people said they felt and what one brain area did. That's a strength.

## What this study can't tell us

It was one small study, with 38 healthy adults and one walk each. It can't tell us whether a shorter walk would do the same, how long the change lasts, or whether it was the trees, the quiet, the lack of traffic or all three. Brooding was something people reported on a questionnaire, and the scan showed blood flow in one area, not thoughts. The study also didn't look at anyone with a mental health condition, so it says nothing about one.

## How can you try a walk under trees today?

The walk, as tested, was 90 minutes among trees, away from traffic. That's a big chunk of time, so here's a plan that fits a real day.

1. **Pick your path.** Look at a map for the nearest park, trail or tree-lined greenway that keeps you away from busy roads. Grass and trees are the goal.
2. **Block the time.** Put the walk on your calendar like a meeting, maybe after a long afternoon at your desk. Leave your phone in your pocket if you can.
3. **Notice, don't solve.** When a worry pops up, let it pass and look at something instead, like bark, leaves or a dog pulling its owner along. Then keep walking. This step is our suggestion; the study didn't test it.

The study tested the full 90 minutes. If you can only do less, it's fine to try, but we can't say it would work the same way.

## When might this not help?

A long walk isn't realistic for everyone, and it won't suit every body or schedule. A small nearby park may be all you have, and that's okay, though the study didn't test that.

If brooding lasts for weeks, keeps getting worse, or comes with a low mood that won't lift, talk to a doctor or a therapist. A walk is not a replacement for that help.

## FAQ

### Does walking in nature reduce rumination?

In one small 2015 study in PNAS by Bratman and colleagues, the 19 adults randomly assigned to a 90-minute nature walk reported less brooding afterward than before. The 19 who walked beside a busy road did not. That's one study's finding, not a promise that it works for everyone.

### How long should you walk in nature to feel better?

The study we covered used a 90-minute walk, so that's the only length we can speak to. We don't know whether a shorter walk would give the same effect. Any time outside may still feel good, but it wasn't tested here.

### Is a walk in a park better than a walk on a busy street?

In this study, the people who walked among grass and oak trees reported less brooding after the walk and showed less blood flow in a brain area linked with rumination. The road walkers didn't. The study can't say whether the trees, the quiet or the lack of traffic made the difference.
