Picture two groups of city people, each on a 90-minute walk. One group follows a roaring road, with buses and horns in their ears. The other strolls through grass and oak trees, away from traffic.

Afterward, researchers scanned everyone's brain. Only the tree walkers showed a change. If you're the type who plans to walk under trees after a big dinner instead of sinking into the couch, this one is for you.

## Why does your mind loop on worries on busy city streets?

You know the feeling. You're walking to the train, and the same awkward email replays in your head for the fifth time.

That loop has a name: brooding, or rumination. It's when your mind chews on the same problem without getting anywhere. Noise, crowds and traffic don't cause it, but a busy street gives you very little room to step out of it.

## What happens in the brain when you brood?

One area often linked with brooding is the subgenual prefrontal cortex. It sits behind the forehead, toward the middle of the brain. It's thought to be involved in sad, self-focused thinking, and activity there has been linked with rumination.

Think of a worry loop as a browser tab you forgot to close. It keeps running in the background while you try to do other things. That's the one image we'll use here, and it's a simplification, since brain areas work in networks and not alone.

## What did the study find about a walk under trees?

In a 2015 study by Bratman and colleagues in the journal PNAS, 38 city adults took a 90-minute walk. Some walked in a natural area with grass and oak trees. Others walked beside a busy road.

After the walk, the nature group reported less brooding. Their brain scans also showed lower activity in the subgenual prefrontal cortex. The road walkers showed no such change.

So here the researchers measured both how people felt and what their brains did. That's a strength.

Still, it was a small study. We don't know yet if a shorter walk would do the same, or whether it was the trees, the quiet, the lack of traffic, or all three. Research suggests a link, not a promise.

## How can you try a walk under trees today?

The habit, as tested, is a 90-minute walk among trees, away from traffic. That's a big chunk of time, so here's a plan that fits a real day.

1. **Pick your path.** Look at a map for the nearest park, trail or tree-lined greenway that keeps you away from busy roads. Grass and trees are the goal.
2. **Block the time.** Put the walk on your calendar like a meeting, maybe right after a heavy meal or a long afternoon at your desk. Leave your phone in your pocket if you can.
3. **Notice, don't solve.** When a worry pops up, let it pass and look at something instead, like bark, leaves or a dog pulling its owner along. Then keep walking.

The study tested the full 90 minutes. If you can only do less, it's fine to try, but we can't say it would work the same way.

## When might this not help?

A long walk isn't realistic for everyone, and it won't suit every body or schedule. A nearby park may be all you have, and that's okay. The study didn't test that.

Brooding that lasts for weeks, keeps getting worse, or comes with a low mood that won't lift can be a sign of depression or anxiety. A walk shouldn't replace help in that case. Talk to a doctor or a therapist.

## FAQ

### Does walking in nature reduce rumination?

One small 2015 study in PNAS by Bratman and colleagues found that 38 adults who took a 90-minute walk in a natural area reported less brooding than those who walked beside a busy road. Research suggests a link, but one small study can't tell us how well it works for everyone.

### How long should you walk in nature to feel better?

The study we covered used a 90-minute walk, so that's the only length we can speak to. We don't know yet whether a shorter walk would give the same effect. Any time outside may still feel good, but it wasn't tested here.

### Is a walk in a park better than a walk on a busy street?

In this study, the people who walked among grass and oak trees showed less brooding and lower activity in a brain area linked with rumination. The road walkers didn't. The study can't say whether the trees, the quiet or the lack of traffic made the difference.
