It's 12:10 a.m. The phone is face down, the lights are off, and your mind starts reading out the list. Email the landlord. Pick up the dry cleaning. Did I answer that message from work? The body is tired, but the brain is running a late-night meeting nobody scheduled.

If that sounds familiar, you are in good company. A bedtime mind full of unfinished business is one of the most common reasons people lie awake. A small experiment suggests a low-tech way to quiet it: write tomorrow's to-do list before bed.

## What the sleep-lab study actually tested

Researchers led by Scullin et al., Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2018, brought young adults into a sleep lab and monitored their sleep overnight. Shortly before lights out, some participants spent 5 minutes writing a detailed to-do list for the coming days. Others spent the same time writing about tasks they had already finished.

The people who wrote the to-do list fell asleep about 9 minutes faster on average. The researchers also looked at how thorough the lists were. The more specific and complete the to-do list, the faster people tended to fall asleep. Writing about completed tasks did not show the same benefit.

This was a single lab study with a specific group, so treat it as a promising idea rather than a guarantee for everyone. Still, the result is practical, cheap, and easy to test on yourself.

## Why offloading your list may help your brain wind down

Unfinished tasks tend to keep nagging at the mind. When you lie in the dark with nothing else to focus on, your brain may keep rehearsing what it is afraid of forgetting. That rehearsal is mental activity, and mental activity is the opposite of drifting off.

One reasonable explanation is that writing the list gives those items somewhere to live other than your head. The planning parts of your brain, including the prefrontal cortex, get a sense that the job has been handled. You are no longer holding the plan in mind, because it is on paper.

That would also explain why the finished-tasks list did not work as well. Looking back at what you have done does not hand off anything for tomorrow. Writing what is coming does.

## How to try it tonight

Keep it simple. You do not need an app or a fancy planner. A pen and a notepad by the bed or on the kitchen table will do.

- **Set a short timer.** Aim for about 5 minutes, the same amount of time used in the study.
- **Write down everything on your mind for tomorrow and the next few days.** Include small errands, emails, calls, and bigger projects.
- **Be specific.** "Email Dana about the invoice before lunch" is better than "work stuff." Detail seemed to matter in the study.
- **Use paper if you can.** A screen may pull you into messages and notifications, which defeats the point.
- **Close the notebook and leave it.** Once the list is written, the job is done for the night.

If a new task pops into your head after you turn off the light, you can jot it on the pad in the dimmest light you can manage. Or tell yourself it is already captured in the morning routine and let it go.

## Making it part of a bigger wind-down

A to-do list is one tool, not a whole sleep plan. It works best alongside habits you may already know: a fairly regular bedtime, a darker and cooler bedroom, and less bright screen time late in the evening. Think of the list as a mental "closing the shop" ritual that signals the workday is over.

It can also help to separate worry from planning. If you notice yourself writing anxious what-ifs instead of tasks, try turning each into a next step, even a tiny one. "Worried about the presentation" becomes "Review slides for 15 minutes after coffee." Concrete steps are easier for the mind to set down than vague dread.

If your sleep trouble is frequent, lasts for weeks, or affects your days, it is worth talking with a doctor or sleep specialist. A notebook is a helpful experiment, not a replacement for professional advice.

## FAQ

### How long should I spend writing my to-do list before bed?

The study used about 5 minutes, which is a good starting point. The goal is to get tomorrow's tasks out of your head and onto paper, so be specific rather than writing for longer.

### Should I write what I did today or what I need to do tomorrow?

In the study, writing about upcoming tasks was linked to falling asleep faster, while writing about completed tasks was not. If you want to try this method, focus on tomorrow and the days ahead.

### Will a bedtime to-do list work for everyone?

Not necessarily. It was one lab study with a specific group, so results may differ for you. It is a low-cost habit worth testing, and if sleep problems persist, talk with a healthcare professional.
