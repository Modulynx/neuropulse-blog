It's mid-afternoon. The third coffee is gone, and now your hands won't sit still on the keyboard. Your jaw is tight, your chest feels a little tight too, and the email you're trying to read keeps sliding out of focus.

If that sounds familiar, a green tea swap might be worth a try. We'll walk through why it may help and where it doesn't.

## Why do my hands shake after too much coffee?

You wanted to feel sharper. Instead you feel wired and a bit off.

That's a fair thing to ask about, and the answer is mostly about adrenaline. Caffeine is linked with a rise in adrenaline, the same hormone your body releases when it thinks something urgent is happening. Adrenaline can make your heart beat faster, your muscles tense, and your hands tremble.

So the shake isn't a sign you're weak or bad at handling stress. It's a normal body response to a lot of caffeine in a short time.

## What is happening in your brain and body?

Think of caffeine as a hard push on a playground swing. One push gets you moving. Three pushes in a row and you're flying higher than you meant to.

Caffeine is thought to block adenosine, a chemical that builds up during the day and makes you feel sleepy. With that signal muted, your body can ramp up its alert systems, including adrenaline. That's helpful for the first cup. By the third, it can tip into jittery.

Green tea has a different mix. It contains some caffeine, but it also has L-theanine, an amino acid found in tea leaves. L-theanine is linked with alpha brain waves, a pattern often seen when people are relaxed but awake. The idea is that it may take the edge off the spike without making you sleepy.

## What does the research say about green tea and L-theanine?

Here we want to be straight with you. We're not pointing to a specific study in this article, so we can't tell you how big any effect is or how long it lasts.

What we can say is the general idea. Researchers have been interested in L-theanine and alpha waves, and in tea as a gentler source of caffeine. That's a reason to try it, not proof that it works for everyone.

Also, green tea still has caffeine. It usually has less than coffee, but if you're very sensitive, you may still feel it.

## How do I try a green tea swap today?

Keep it small. One afternoon is enough to see how your body responds.

1. **Spot your third cup.** Notice the time you usually reach for it. For many people it's around 2 or 3 pm, right when energy dips.
2. **Swap it for green tea.** Steep a mug and let it cool a little. Sip it slowly, with a glass of water nearby, instead of pouring more coffee.
3. **Check in after a while.** Look at your hands, your jaw, your chest and your focus. Jot down one line in your phone about how you felt.

If you like it, make the swap a regular afternoon habit. If you don't notice a difference, that's useful information too.

## When might this not help?

A green tea swap isn't a treatment. If you're sensitive to caffeine, green tea may still leave you jittery, and a caffeine-free herbal tea might suit you better.

Shaky hands, a racing heart or chest tightness that happen often, show up even with little caffeine, or get worse over time deserve a conversation with a doctor. If the wired feeling comes with constant worry or trouble sleeping, a therapist or doctor can help you look at the bigger picture. And if your chest pain is severe or sudden, get medical help right away.

## FAQ

### Why do my hands shake after drinking coffee?

Caffeine is linked with a rise in adrenaline, a hormone that can make your heart beat faster and your muscles tense. That can show up as shaky hands, especially after several cups. If it happens often or with small amounts of caffeine, talk to a doctor.

### Does green tea have less caffeine than coffee?

Green tea usually has less caffeine than a cup of coffee, though it varies by brand and how long you steep it. It still contains caffeine, so very sensitive people may still feel jittery.

### Can L-theanine in green tea help with coffee jitters?

L-theanine is linked with alpha brain waves, a pattern often seen in relaxed but alert states, so the idea is that it may smooth out caffeine's edge. We aren't citing a specific study here, so think of it as something to try, not a promise.
