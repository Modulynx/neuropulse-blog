It's almost 10 on a Tuesday night. The dishes are done, a mug is still on the counter, and something feels off. Not sad, exactly. Not angry. Just a low hum.

Then, brushing your teeth, a word shows up: *dreading*. You're dreading the 9 am meeting. And the hum gets a little quieter.

## Why does one word change how a feeling feels?

An unnamed feeling is slippery. It tints the whole evening and you can't tell what it's attached to.

Once it has a word, it has edges. It becomes something you notice instead of something that runs you. Psychologists call this "affect labeling," which just means putting a feeling into words.

## What happens in your brain when you name a feeling?

Deep in your brain sits the amygdala. Think of it as a smoke alarm: quick, loud, and happy to go off for burnt toast.

Behind your forehead is the prefrontal cortex, which helps with language and with stepping back from a situation. The idea is that finding a word pulls in this front part of the brain, and that goes with a calmer alarm.

## What did the study find?

A team at UCLA led by Matthew Lieberman put volunteers in an fMRI scanner and showed them upsetting pictures ([Lieberman et al., Psychological Science, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x)). Sometimes people picked a word for the emotion in the picture. Other times they handled the same kind of picture in a different way, without naming the feeling.

When people named the emotion, the amygdala and other emotion areas responded less to the pictures. One area got more active: a spot on the right side of the prefrontal cortex. And the more that spot lit up, the less the amygdala did.

## What this study can't tell us

A brain scan shows activity, not feelings. This study looked at pictures in a lab, not at your Tuesday night, so it can't say how well naming works when you're exhausted or dealing with something big. It also can't say how long any calming lasts.

## How to try naming a feeling today

Keep it to one word. Not a paragraph, not a diagnosis.

1. **Pause when you notice the hum.** While the coffee brews, or before you open that email.
2. **Pick one word.** *Nervous*, *tired*, *annoyed*, *lonely*, *dreading*. If it feels off, swap it until it fits.
3. **Say it or write it.** Whisper it, or type it in your notes app. Then carry on with your day.

You don't have to fix the feeling. You're only giving it a name.

## Who should be careful?

For most people this is a low-risk habit. If you live with depression, trauma or an anxiety disorder, a word on a sticky note doesn't replace care, and naming a strong feeling can sting at first. Go gently and talk with a licensed professional if it gets heavy. And if the "feeling" is physical, like chest pain or trouble breathing, don't label it as stress. Get it checked.

## FAQ

### Does naming your emotions really calm you down?

In the study above, naming the emotion in a picture went with less amygdala activity. That's a link seen in a scanner, not a promise that you'll feel calm every time.

### What is affect labeling?

Putting a feeling into words, like thinking "I'm nervous." It's a way of noticing an emotion instead of letting it run in the background.

### What if I can't tell what I'm feeling?

That's common. Start with a rough word like *tired* or *tense*, then change it if a better one comes. If it's hard most of the time, a therapist can help you build the skill.
